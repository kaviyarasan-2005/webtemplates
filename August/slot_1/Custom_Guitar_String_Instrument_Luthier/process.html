The Making Of

Anatomy of a Build

It takes approximately 120 hours to build a FRET instrument. Discover the meticulous steps that transform raw timber into a resonant voice.

Material

The Tonewood Vault

Everything begins with the wood. We ethically source and naturally air-dry our materials for years before they ever touch a saw blade.

Adirondack Spruce

The legendary top wood. Unrivaled for its high stiffness-to-weight ratio, providing massive headroom and dynamic range.

Honduran Mahogany

Stable, lightweight, and resonant. We use genuine Honduran mahogany for our necks and electric bodies to ensure structural integrity and a warm midrange punch.

Indian Rosewood

The gold standard for acoustic backs and sides. Deep, resonant lows and glassy, bell-like highs with complex overtones.

Step 01

Selection & Voicing

No two pieces of wood are identical. They possess different densities, stiffness, and resonant frequencies.

Before carving begins, we "tap-tune" the raw boards. By holding the wood at specific nodes and striking it, the luthier listens to the fundamental pitch and overtones. This dictates how thin the top will be sanded and how the bracing will be carved to achieve the desired voice.

Step 02

Bracing the Soundboard

The braces act as the architectural ribs of the instrument, controlling how energy travels from the strings across the top.

Using traditional hot hide glue, the braces are attached and then scalloped by hand using small finger planes and chisels. The luthier continuously taps and flexes the top, removing shavings of spruce until the perfect balance of structural stability and acoustic responsiveness is achieved.

Step 03

Carving the Neck

The neck is the physical connection between the player and the instrument. It must feel like an extension of the hand.

We completely eschew automated CNC profiling. Every FRET neck is roughed out on a bandsaw and then painstakingly shaped by hand using spoke shaves, rasps, and scrapers. This allows the luthier to follow the grain and create a fluid, organic taper that feels instantly familiar.

Step 04

Finishing & Setup

A thick finish suffocates tone. We utilize ultra-thin nitrocellulose lacquer, applied over weeks.

After the finish cures, it is hand-rubbed to a glass-like gloss or a smooth satin feel. Finally, the frets are leveled to a tolerance of a thousandth of an inch, the nut slots are filed, and the saddle is compensated to ensure perfect intonation across the entire fretboard.

Advanced Craft

Specialized Build Techniques

Every FRET instrument benefits from centuries-old techniques refined with modern precision. These are the methods that set our instruments apart.

Acoustic

Tap-Tuning & Voicing

Each soundboard is tuned by hand to a target frequency by removing material from the braces. This process, called "voicing," determines the instrument's fundamental character — whether it's a cannon or a whisper.

Structural

Hand-Scalloped Bracing

Rather than using CNC-routed braces, every brace is hand-scalloped using finger planes and chisels. This allows the luthier to respond to each piece of wood's unique characteristics and optimize vibration transfer.

Ergonomic

Hand-Carved Neck Profiles

Every neck is shaped by hand using spoke shaves, rasps, and scrapers. This creates an organic, flowing taper that machine-produced necks simply cannot replicate — the neck feels like an extension of your hand.

Expert Guidance

When Should You Consult a Luthier?

Not every issue requires professional intervention — but some problems, if ignored, can cause irreversible damage. Here are the signs it's time to bring your instrument to a specialist.

Persistent Fret Buzz

Buzz that doesn't go away after a string change often indicates uneven frets, a warped neck, or an incorrect relief setting.

Visible Cracks or Separation

Cracks in the top, back, or binding, and any separating seams need immediate professional attention before they worsen.

Intonation Issues

If your guitar plays in tune open but goes sharp or flat up the neck, the nut, saddle, or fret placement may need professional adjustment.

Strange Noises or Rattles

Loose braces, lifting bridges, or failing hardware can create internal rattles that degrade your tone and threaten structural integrity.

High Action After Years of Play

Gradually rising action is often a sign of belly bulge, a worn saddle, or neck angle issues — all of which require expert diagnosis.

Player Education

Guitar Care & Maintenance Tips

Proper care extends the life of your instrument and keeps it playing and sounding its best. Here are the essentials every player should know.

Humidity Control

Wood breathes. Keep your instrument at 45–55% relative humidity to prevent cracking and warping.

Use a soundhole humidifier in dry climates
Never store near heaters or A/C vents

Cleaning & Polish

Wipe your strings and body after each session. A simple microfiber cloth removes oils that corrode strings and dull finishes.

Use a dry microfiber cloth after every session
Avoid silicone-based polishes on lacquer finishes

Regular String Changes

Dead strings sound dull and can develop rough spots that wear frets faster. Change strings every 30–60 hours of play.

Change one string at a time to maintain tension
Don't leave a guitar unstrung for extended periods

Proper Storage

Always store your instrument in a hardshell case when not playing. Avoid attics, basements, and car trunks — temperature extremes are the enemy.

Store in a quality hardshell case at room temp
Never leave in direct sunlight or a hot car

Seasonal Setup Checks

Wood moves with humidity changes. A bi-annual professional setup keeps your action, relief, and intonation dialed in year-round.

Schedule setups before winter and summer
Don't force truss rod adjustments yourself

Fretboard Conditioning

Unfinished rosewood and ebony fretboards need conditioning oil 1–2 times per year to prevent drying, shrinkage, and sharp fret ends.

Apply lemon oil or fretboard conditioner sparingly
Never use household oils or cooking oils
