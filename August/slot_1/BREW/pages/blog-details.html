The Perfect IPA: A Step-by-Step Guide

Sep 2, 2026 Recipes 8 min read

Choosing Your Hops

The hop selection is the defining decision in any IPA recipe. Hops contribute bitterness, flavor, and aroma — and in an IPA, you want all three working in harmony. The key is understanding the difference between bittering hops, flavor hops, and aroma hops, and knowing when to add each during the boil.

For a classic American IPA, you will want to start with a clean, high-alpha bittering hop at 60 minutes, then layer in expressive American or New World hops as the boil progresses. Late additions — at 15 minutes, 5 minutes, and flameout — are where the magic happens. These additions preserve volatile aromatic compounds that would otherwise boil off.

Dry hopping is the final flourish. Adding hops to the fermenter after primary fermentation slows down extracts intense tropical, citrus, and piney aromas without contributing additional bitterness. Many modern IPAs rely heavily on generous dry hop charges to achieve that signature juicy nose.

  • Citra — Bright citrus and tropical fruit notes. Excellent for late additions and dry hopping. One of the most versatile IPA hops available.
  • Mosaic — Complex berry, mango, and herbal character. Pairs beautifully with Citra for layered aromatics and a deep, resinous backbone.
  • Simcoe — Earthy pine with underlying stone fruit. Works well as both a bittering and aroma hop, giving your IPA structural depth.
  • Galaxy — Passion fruit and peach with a hint of citrus. An Australian hop that brings a distinctive Southern Hemisphere character to hazy and West Coast styles alike.

Water Profile

Water chemistry is the silent architect behind every great IPA. The mineral content of your brewing water directly affects mash pH, hop extraction efficiency, and the final flavor profile of your beer. For IPAs, the goal is a water profile that emphasizes hop bitterness and crispness.

Start by testing your source water. Most municipal water can be adjusted with additions of gypsum (calcium sulfate) to emphasize hop character, and a small amount of calcium chloride can round out the malt body. The recommended sulfate-to-chloride ratio for a West Coast IPA is approximately 2:1, which pushes hop bitterness to the forefront.

Target a mash pH between 5.2 and 5.4 for optimal enzyme activity during the mash. Use lactic acid or acidulated malt to adjust downward if needed. Keep total mineral content below 250 ppm to avoid harsh or minerally off-flavors. Water adjustment is subtle but transformative — once you dial in your profile, you will notice a dramatic improvement in the clarity and balance of your finished beer.

Mash Schedule

The mash is where starches in your grain bill convert to fermentable sugars. For an IPA, a single-infusion mash at 150°F (65.5°C) for 60 minutes strikes the right balance between fermentability and body. This temperature favors beta-amylase activity, producing a drier, more fermentable wort that lets hops shine.

If you prefer a slightly fuller body and residual sweetness to counterbalance aggressive hopping, raise the mash temperature to 152°F–154°F (67°C–68°C). A mash-out at 168°F (76°C) for 10 minutes halts enzymatic activity and improves lauterability, making it easier to separate sweet wort from spent grain.

Measure your original gravity (OG) before the boil. A typical American IPA targets an OG of 1.060–1.075, which will yield an ABV in the 6.0–7.5% range after full attenuation. Record these numbers in your brew log — consistency comes from tracking and replicating what works.

The Boil

A vigorous 60-minute boil serves multiple purposes: it sterilizes the wort, drives off unwanted volatile compounds like dimethyl sulfide (DMS), concentrates the gravity, and isosomerizes hop alpha acids to create bitterness. For IPAs, the hop schedule during the boil is where you build layers of flavor and bitterness.

The typical hop addition schedule for a well-balanced IPA follows a staggered approach. Add a clean bittering hop at the start of the boil (60 minutes) to establish your IBU foundation. At 15 minutes, add flavor hops. At 5 minutes and at flameout (0 minutes), add your most expressive aroma hops. Consider a whirlpool or hop stand at 170°F (77°C) for 20–30 minutes after the boil to extract maximum aroma without adding harsh bitterness.

Irish moss or whirlloc tablets added in the final 15 minutes of the boil help clarify the finished beer by coagulating proteins. Keep the boil at a rolling but controlled pace — a boil that is too vigorous can cause boil-overs and drive off desirable hop compounds.

"The secret to a great IPA isn't just about hops — it's about balance."

Fermentation

Yeast selection and temperature control are what separate a good IPA from a great one. For a clean, classic American IPA, use a neutral ale yeast such as Safale US-05 or White Labs WLP001 (California Ale). These strains attenuate aggressively, producing a dry finish that lets hop character dominate.

Pitch your yeast at 64°F–66°F (18°C–19°C) and allow the temperature to rise naturally to 68°F–72°F (20°C–22°C) over the first three to four days. This controlled temperature ramp encourages healthy fermentation while minimizing the production of fusel alcohols and esters that can muddy the hop profile.

For hazy or New England IPAs, consider a strain like London Ale III (WLP013) or Imperial A38 Juice, which produce stone fruit esters and a soft, pillowy mouthfeel. These strains pair with large dry hop additions during active fermentation — a technique known as biotransformation dry hopping — to create the signature juicy haze that defines the style.

Fermentation typically takes 10–14 days. Once terminal gravity is reached and stable for three consecutive days, you are ready to package. Whether you keg or bottle, cold conditioning for an additional 7–14 days will smooth out any rough edges and allow the flavors to meld into a cohesive, balanced IPA.
